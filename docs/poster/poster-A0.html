Question and hypotheses

A leak opens and only four pressure sensors can see it. How many wasted digs (“dry holes”) does a crew need, and does a hydraulic model of the network help? Written down before any method was run (Git history shows the order).

H1 Model fitting needs at least 50% fewer dry holes than the naive rule.PARTIALLY SUPPORTED
Observed 13.6% fewer (95% interval 10.0% to 17.2%).
H2 The cheap fingerprint method is within 5 points of model fitting and 10 times faster.PARTIALLY SUPPORTED
8,035 times faster, but the gap is 3.5 points (upper bound 6.6).

The targets were missed and were not changed afterwards.

Method

A simulated city: 13.4 km of pipe, 14 junctions, 6 loops, 4 pressure sensors (squares). Hazen–Williams hydraulics, Global Gradient Algorithm. 1,000 random leaks of 5–25 L/s; the methods do not know the real demand (±10% per junction); sensor error 0.10 m. Four methods choose where to dig, with the same crew rule.

Results

Mean dry holes before the leak is found (95% interval); fewer is better; 10 = not found.

Model fitting never found 38.8% of the leaks in 10 digs. Fingerprints take 0.03 ms; model fitting takes 243 ms.

What decides the result

Dry holes saved against the naive rule. With demand known exactly, fingerprints save 41%; with twice the uncertainty, 1%. Knowing the demand matters more than the algorithm.

Limits and honesty

  • A simulation of a drawn network: no real leaks, sensors or Moldovan data.
  • The model is assumed exact; one leak; steady flow.
  • Low-cost transmitters (±0.5–1% of full scale = 0.5–1 m on 10 bar) are noisier than the 0.10 m assumed.
  • Next: a real district, a flow meter at the plant, several leaks.
